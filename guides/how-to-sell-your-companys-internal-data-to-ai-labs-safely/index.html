Publishers, music and stock libraries, archives, and reference-data companies are finding that AI labs will license what they hold, including the metadata around it. The sellers who do well are the ones who can answer a buyer's diligence questions with records. This guide covers what labs license, where sales go wrong, and how to prepare a catalog.

What company data do AI labs license?

AI labs license content catalogs (text, audio, video, images, and structured data) and the metadata that describes them: descriptive records, cue sheets, taxonomies, annotations, and usage statistics. Metadata makes a corpus findable and groundable, and rebuilding expert-authored metadata is slow and expensive. Labs also license internal records that show how work gets done, such as support conversations and workflow data, which carry the extra risks covered below.

Metadata deserves its own line in any sale. It can be licensed for ingestion into training, for display and retrieval in AI answers, or for enriching other data, and each use carries a different value. A seller who prices the content and ignores the metadata leaves part of the asset on the table.

Who owns the metadata in a content catalog?

Metadata ownership is often split. A platform owns the data it generates from its own operations, such as search counts and usage statistics. Descriptive records that came from an individual supplier, library, or contributor usually belong to that contributor. An aggregate statistic stays on the platform side only if it does not reproduce any individual contributor's record. That split decides who gets paid and who stands behind the rights.

Before a sale, map each metadata layer to its owner and to the agreement behind that ownership: supplier contracts, contributor terms, platform terms, or employment. Record who holds AI-training and sublicensing rights for each layer. If a buyer licenses only part of the catalog, note how payment is prorated across the records actually used.

How do you prepare a content catalog for sale to AI labs?

Inventory the catalog and every metadata layer. Map who owns each layer and the source of that ownership. Record what rights and consent cover AI training, and where the paperwork says nothing. Keep a written register of anything you or your counsel exclude. Get a valuation that prices content and metadata separately. Then assemble a machine-readable manifest that a buyer's diligence team can read without rework.

  1. Inventory. List the collections, formats, and metadata layers, and where each one lives.
  2. Ownership map. For each layer, name the owner and the contract or term behind that ownership.
  3. Rights and consent. Record what each agreement, license, or notice says about AI training and sublicensing, and note every place it is silent.
  4. Exclusions. Keep a written register of excluded works or record classes and who decided each exclusion.
  5. Valuation. Value content and metadata separately, by use (ingestion, display and retrieval, enrichment), with the method stated.
  6. Manifest. Package collections, layers, owners, rights basis, and exclusions in a machine-readable format for the data room.

What are the risks of selling internal company archives?

Internal archives such as email, chat, support logs, and HR records carry more risk than content catalogs. Employee and customer records can be re-identified when they are linked, even after names are removed, and collection notices rarely covered AI training or sale. A seller of an internal archive needs independent de-identification testing and a documented register of excluded record classes before any buyer sees the data.

In a 2026 airline archive sale, a flight attendants' union argued publicly that linked records could expose discipline, medical accommodation, and grievance files, and asked that employee data be excluded or reviewed separately. A buyer's promise to remove such records later is weaker than a seller's documented exclusion now, which is why Credtent is building an archive review that includes independent de-identification testing.
